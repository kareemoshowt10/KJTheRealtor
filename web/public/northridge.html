The Long Story

A town named for a well, built up around a racetrack.

Most agents will tell you what a neighborhood costs. Almost none know that its name once meant "oasis," or that the 40 acres now covered in lecture halls used to host Sunday harness races. Northridge has reinvented its own ground at least three times — each version left something real behind for the next one.

“A community built on a water-well name has never had trouble finding reasons to grow.”

1908 Zelzah — a name that means oasis

Settlers founded the community as Zelzah, a biblical word for a place with a life-giving water well. It was quiet San Fernando Valley farmland for two more decades — citrus groves and open fields, a rail stop, and a name meant to promise water in a dry valley.

1929 Renamed North Los Angeles

The Southern Pacific Railroad station and the surrounding community were renamed North Los Angeles — a name that immediately confused mail, deliveries, and directions with North Hollywood on the other side of the Valley. It didn’t last a decade.

Nov. 1, 1938 Northridge, finally

Civic leader Carl S. Dentzel led the push to rename the community Northridge Village, which shortened into the name that stuck. Two renames in ten years is unusual even by Valley standards — a sign of a place that kept outgrowing whatever it was called.

1943–1948 Devonshire Downs opens

Forty acres at Devonshire Street and Zelzah Avenue became a harness-racing track in 1943, with weekly Sunday races starting in 1946. The State of California bought the property in 1948 and made it home to the San Fernando Valley Fair — the neighborhood’s first real gathering place.

1956–1958 A college breaks ground

Ceremonial groundbreaking for a new San Fernando Valley campus happened January 4, 1956, on former agricultural land nearby. It opened that fall as a branch of Los Angeles State College, then separated into its own school — San Fernando Valley State College — on July 1, 1958.

1972 Renamed California State University, Northridge

The college took the neighborhood’s name for good. Devonshire Downs, by then hosting concerts, swap meets, and expos as much as horse racing, was eventually absorbed by the growing campus and is now CSUN’s North Campus — the racetrack quietly became a university.

Jan. 17, 1994 The quake

A magnitude-6.7 earthquake struck at 4:31 a.m., centered near Reseda Boulevard and Arminta Street, a few minutes from Northridge’s own streets. It was devastating, and it was also the event that rewrote seismic retrofit standards across California — a hard lesson the whole state still builds on.

Today Growing again

CSUN now enrolls roughly 38,000 students on a 356-acre campus. A few blocks away, new apartment construction is rising on the mall property. Northridge keeps doing what it’s done since 1908 — finding a reason to grow into whatever comes next.

Field Notes

What locals know that listings don’t say.

For investors

A 38,000-student demand engine

Streets near Zelzah Avenue, Reseda Boulevard, and Nordhoff Street stay in near-constant turnover from CSUN students, grad students, and staff. Well-kept units close to campus rarely sit vacant long — a rental floor most Valley neighborhoods don’t have.

For the historians

The racetrack under the lecture halls

CSUN’s North Campus sits on the old Devonshire Downs fairground — harness races, then the San Fernando Valley Fair, then decades of concerts and swap meets before the university absorbed it. Nothing above ground hints at it now, which is exactly the kind of layered history I like showing clients.

For the practical

Ask about the retrofit history

The 1994 quake was centered close enough that it’s part of this area’s honest story, not something to gloss over. What it means today: foundation bolting and cripple-wall retrofits are a standard diligence item on older homes here, the same as almost anywhere in this part of the Valley. I pull that history before you write an offer.

For the commuters

The 118 is right there

The Ronald Reagan Freeway runs along Northridge’s northern edge, with Devonshire, Nordhoff, Roscoe, and Reseda Boulevard forming the working grid underneath it. No neighborhood mystique required — it’s a genuinely easy commute from most of the 91324 pocket.

For the growth-watchers

New construction at the mall

A 4.78-acre piece of the Northridge Fashion Center property is becoming roughly 350 new apartment units, with construction underway. Real, funded investment in the neighborhood’s commercial core — the kind of signal I flag for clients thinking five years out, not five weeks.

The Analytics

How to read the Northridge market like a local.

Northridge isn’t one market — it’s a handful of distinct pockets built around different anchors, each pricing and renting differently. Here is the honest map.

The demand engine

The CSUN corridor

Zelzah Avenue, Reseda Boulevard, and Nordhoff Street closest to campus — the streets where student and staff rental demand runs steadiest. New off-campus housing keeps landing here, a sign institutional money still sees runway.

The family grid

The Calahan & 91324 core

Quiet postwar residential streets west of campus, anchored by neighborhood schools like Calahan Street Elementary. Calmer lease demand, more owner-occupants, and the pocket I know best through family rather than a spreadsheet.

The growth zone

The mall corridor

The blocks around Shirley Avenue and the Northridge Fashion Center, where roughly 350 new apartment units are under construction. Watch this pocket for the next few years — new supply usually pulls new retail and new comps behind it.

The steady core

The Tampa & Devonshire heart

Central Northridge near the weekly farmers market — a genuine mix of housing vintages, walkable to daily errands, and about as close to "default Northridge" as the neighborhood gets.

The northern edge

Porter Ranch-adjacent streets

Northridge’s pockets nearest the 118 and the Porter Ranch line pick up some of that newer, more master-planned feel while staying priced like Northridge. A frequent value play for buyers priced out of Porter Ranch itself.

The value line

The Reseda Boulevard border

The western edge, where Northridge and Reseda share character and comps. Similar housing stock to the CSUN corridor at a gentler price, for buyers who want the university-adjacent rental demand without the premium.

The Calendar

A year in Northridge.

The standing dates that turn a campus town into a hometown.

Wednesdays, March–Oct

The Northridge Farmers’ Market & Family Festival

Tampa Avenue closes out its weekday evenings, 5 to 9 p.m., for local produce, food stalls, and small-business booths. It’s the neighborhood’s most reliable weekly gathering, and the easiest way to feel the pulse of central Northridge.

Late August

Fall move-in at CSUN

Roughly 38,000 students cycle back through the corridor around campus, and off-campus rental demand spikes accordingly. Owners near Zelzah, Reseda, and Nordhoff plan their turnover around this window — and so should anyone buying a rental here.

Spring

CSUN commencement season

Graduation traffic fills the corridor for a few weeks each spring, and it’s a genuinely good excuse to walk the campus streets and see the neighborhood at its most alive, before the summer rental turnover begins.

Straight Answers

The questions people actually ask me about Northridge.

Answered the way I answer everything — even when the honest version costs me.

Is Northridge a good place to buy or lease?

Yes, and for a reason a lot of buyers underweight: CSUN. A 356-acre university with roughly 38,000 students and 4,000-plus faculty and staff sits at the neighborhood’s core, which means steady rental demand, real daytime economic activity, and a buyer pool that includes staff, faculty, and families who want to be close without living on campus. Add direct access to the 118 and a genuinely wide mix of postwar family streets and newer construction near the mall corridor, and you get more underlying demand drivers than the neighborhood’s reputation usually gets credit for.

Is the 1994 earthquake still something I should worry about buying here?

The Northridge quake was real, it was serious, and I won’t pretend otherwise — a magnitude-6.7 event centered a few minutes from here that reshaped how California inspects and retrofits buildings, especially older wood-frame homes with cripple walls and unbolted foundations. The honest, practical version for a buyer today: that same event is why retrofit history and foundation bolting are now a standard, expected part of diligence on any older home in this part of the Valley — not a Northridge-specific red flag, a Southern-California-wide one.

I pull retrofit and permit history before you write an offer, the same way I would for myself.

How does CSUN affect the rental market here?

Significantly, and mostly in an owner’s favor. Streets near Zelzah Avenue, Reseda Boulevard, and Nordhoff Street see steady turnover from students, grad students, and staff, which keeps well-kept units renting quickly close to campus. Move a few blocks into the quieter 91324 grid — the pocket around Calahan Street, for instance — and you get calmer, more family-oriented lease demand instead. Knowing which few blocks you’re actually buying into changes the strategy, and I’ll tell you which one a specific address sits in before you commit to a plan.

Northridge or Porter Ranch — which is the better buy?

They’re not really competing for the same buyer. Porter Ranch is newer, more master-planned, and priced for that; Northridge is older housing stock with more variation street to street, a real university anchor, and typically more house and land per dollar. If the plan is turnkey and uniform, Porter Ranch usually wins. If the plan is value, character, and proximity to a major institution that keeps demand steady, Northridge is frequently the stronger instrument — I show both regularly and will run the actual numbers for your situation rather than a generic answer.

What’s happening with the Northridge Fashion Center redevelopment?

The mall itself — one of the largest in Los Angeles County — is staying put, but a 4.78-acre piece of the property is being developed into roughly 350 new apartment units, with construction underway and delivery expected in the next few years. That’s real, funded new investment landing in the neighborhood’s commercial core, and it’s exactly the kind of quiet growth signal I watch for clients thinking about this area’s next five years, not just this week’s comps.
