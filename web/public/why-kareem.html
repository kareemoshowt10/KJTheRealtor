The long game

Thirty years, not thirty days.

I call myself a Generational Wealth Realtor because that's the actual job. I'm an agent of families — my work is generational wealth preservation and creation, helping you acquire, structure, and hold property so it strengthens your family for decades, not just closes in thirty days.

That's why I talk about normalizing the family compound: families living near each other, pooling capital, keeping equity inside the family instead of handing it away one closing at a time. The modern American dream quietly sold us on isolation — everyone in their own house, on their own mortgage, scattered across cities, so every generation starts over from zero. It is the most expensive way a family has ever been asked to live.

It was not always like this. Family compounds — parents, children, and cousins on shared or adjacent land — are how most of the world has always built stability. Pooled resources, shared childcare and eldercare, and land that passes down instead of being sold off to a stranger. Done right, it is the single most resilient, tax-aware, and durable way for a normal family to build real wealth.

The money conversation

How I actually get paid.

Most agents make you ask. The rules changed materially in 2024 and 2025, most explanations of them online are wrong in the same specific way, and you are entitled to understand this before you sign anything.

The rate is negotiable — always was

No law, no association, and no brokerage sets it. California has long required that any compensation provision be immediately preceded by a notice, in at least 10-point boldface, saying exactly that: commissions are not fixed by law and are set by each broker individually. As of AB 2992, a rate can’t even be pre-printed on the form — it has to be filled in as a negotiated term.

What changed in August 2024

Under the NAR settlement, effective August 17, 2024, offers of compensation to buyer brokers came off the MLS, and Realtors must have a written agreement with a buyer before touring a home. Worth being precise: that is trade-association and MLS policy binding Realtors, not a California statute. Plenty of articles call it “the new law.” It isn’t.

What California law actually requires

AB 2992 took effect January 1, 2025. It requires a written buyer-broker representation agreement executed as soon as practicable and no later than when you sign your offer, and it caps the term at three months for individual buyers. So the statute ties the deadline to the offer; the before-touring rule comes from the Realtor side. Both apply to me.

Sellers can still contribute

Removing compensation from the MLS did not stop sellers from paying it. It just moved it into negotiation instead of advertisement. A seller can still pay or contribute toward your agent’s compensation, and you can ask for credits in your offer. Anything paid by someone else is credited against what you owe under your agreement with me.

The disclosure comes first

Before you sign a buyer-broker agreement with me, you get the Disclosure Regarding Real Estate Agency Relationship — the document explaining who represents whom and what each role owes you. I’d rather you read it closely and ask hard questions than initial it politely.

What this means in practice

You will know the number, in writing, before you are committed to anything — and you can negotiate it. If the way I’m paid ever creates a conflict with the advice I’m giving you, I will name the conflict out loud. That is the entire point of the four beliefs above.

Do I have to give you my email to read your guides?

What is a "family compound," exactly?

Two or more generations of one family living on shared or adjacent property they own together — adjacent lots, a main home with ADUs, a duplex, or a large-lot estate. Structured so grandparents, parents, and kids live close without living on top of each other, and held so the land passes down instead of being sold off. The next generation inherits a launchpad instead of a fresh thirty-year mortgage.

Do you only work with families planning compounds?

No. Most of my work is ordinary buying and selling done carefully. The compound is where the thinking leads if a family wants it — it's not a prerequisite for working together. If you want to buy one house and live in it for forty years, that's a generational wealth strategy too, and I'll treat it like one.

Are you a tax advisor or an attorney?

No, and I don't pretend to be. Everything I publish is educational, not legal, tax, financial, or lending advice. What I do is hold the real estate side of the plan and coordinate with your estate attorney and CPA so the pieces line up instead of contradicting each other.
