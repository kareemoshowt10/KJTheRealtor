The first 30 days

A sequence, not a scramble.

Work from the top. The later steps get easier when the earlier ones are true.

01

Locate the will, the trust, and the deed

Before anything is moved, find the documents that say how the house is owned and who is allowed to act. Look for the original will, any living trust and its amendments, and the recorded deed. Set aside the latest property-tax bill, the insurance policy, mortgage or home-equity statements, and a death certificate when you have one.

Check the home files, a safe-deposit box, and the office of the attorney who drafted the estate plan. Keep old envelopes. A copy of a will is not always enough, and a trust that was never funded may not hold the house. Do not throw papers away because they look old.

02

See whether this is probate or trust administration

A will names who should receive property. In California it does not, by itself, transfer the deed or skip court. If the home is titled in the person’s name alone, the estate often goes through probate: a court process to settle what is owed and pass title.

If the home is titled in a trust, a successor trustee usually handles that asset through trust administration. That work has its own notices and duties. It is not a casual family agreement, and it is not the same thing as probate. The deed, the trust, and the will decide the path. A probate attorney reads them. California also has simplified procedures for some smaller estates. Whether this house qualifies is a legal question.

03

Secure the property and talk to the insurer

Keep the house locked. Know who has keys, garage openers, and alarm codes. Confirm who has authority before you change locks or remove belongings. Leave the utilities the house needs so it is not neglected while title is being settled. Photograph the condition. Forward the mail once you are allowed to.

Call the insurance company. Say the owner has died, and ask whether a vacant home is still covered and what the policy requires. Vacancy can limit or end coverage. If a loan exists, find the servicer and ask what must stay current. Do not assume the old policy or the old payment rhythm still fits.

04

Do not rush the decision

The first month is for documents, protection, and a picture the heirs share. Listing, renting, renovating, or buying out a sibling before title, debts, and taxes are understood is how families create a second problem. Disagreement in a hard month is normal. It is not a reason to skip the paperwork.

You can learn the condition of the house, and what similar homes nearby have sold for, without putting a sign in the yard. If the house is full of belongings, hear the people who inherit them before anything is donated or hauled away. A separate walkthrough covers the clean-out conversation.

05

Name the options. Choose later.

Most heir situations land on one of five paths: keep the home, rent it, sell it as-is, prepare it and then sell, or arrange a sibling buyout. Each has carrying costs, family consequences, and tax consequences. Write them on one page together.

You do not owe anyone a choice in the first 30 days. A clear list is the decision. The pick can wait until the attorney and the CPA have seen the file.

06

Take two tax questions to the right people

Ask a CPA how federal income-tax basis works if this home is sold, including what people call a step-up in basis. Ask a probate attorney and a tax advisor how Proposition 19’s parent-child exclusion applies if a child may keep the home. Those two answers change the cost of keeping, renting, or selling.

They are not answers a real estate agent is licensed to give. The official explanations are below, so you can read them before those meetings. The meetings still matter.

Two different jobs

Probate and trust administration are not interchangeable.

A family can have a will and a trust. The house follows the document that actually holds title.

Court

Probate

Probate is the court process for settling an estate and transferring assets that were in the decedent’s own name. A will guides the court. It does not replace it. Creditors, notices, and a personal representative are part of that process. Timing and procedure belong to the attorney and the court, not to a checklist.

Trustee

Trust administration

When the deed is in the name of a trust, the successor trustee generally administers that asset outside a full probate of the house. The trustee still has duties: notices, an accounting the beneficiaries can understand, and a transfer or sale done with authority. “It’s in the trust” is the start of the question, not the end of it.

The five paths

Keep, rent, sell, or buy one another out.

None of these is automatically the right one. They are the menu, so the family is arguing about the same list.

01

Keep

An heir, or the family together, lives in the house. That is a life decision and, in California, often a property-tax decision. Proposition 19 treats a child who makes the home a principal residence differently from a child who does not. Read the Board of Equalization page below, then ask a tax advisor before anyone moves in on an assumption.

02

Rent

Holding the house as a rental means carrying costs, a landlord’s legal duties, and often a different property-tax result than moving in. Insurance for a rented home is its own policy question. Do not advertise it, or promise it to a tenant, until the person with authority and a tax advisor have said the hold makes sense.

03

Sell as-is

The house is offered in its current condition, with the disclosures the sale requires. Families consider this when the work is more than they want to manage, or when a simpler close matters more than a prepared sale. Condition is still described honestly. As-is is a pricing and scope choice, not a way around disclosure.

04

Prepare and sell

Some houses benefit from a defined scope of work before they are listed: cleaning, the repairs a buyer’s inspector would flag, and a calm presentation. That scope should be a real bid, agreed by the people who have authority, after the family has already decided to sell. Preparing a house you might keep is a different project.

05

Sibling buyout

One heir purchases the others’ interests and the house stays with someone who already loves it. It needs an agreed value, a way to fund it, and documents a lawyer prepares. A buyout done on a handshake is how siblings stop speaking. A buyout done on paper can be the fairest of the five paths.

Read the source, then call a professional

Two rules heirs hear about, and where they actually live.

High level only. The numbers and the exceptions for this house belong to a CPA and, for property tax, to the county assessor and a tax advisor.

Federal income tax

Step-up in basis

For federal income tax, the basis of property inherited from a decedent is generally its fair market value on the date of death. If the estate qualifies and the personal representative elects it, basis can instead be the value on an alternate valuation date. People call the change from the decedent’s old cost a step-up in basis. It can change the taxable gain if the home is later sold.

It is not universal. Property given to the decedent shortly before death can be treated differently, and some estates must keep the heir’s basis consistent with the estate-tax value. Community-property and trust details can change the result. The IRS explains inherited basis in Publication 551 and in its gifts-and-inheritances FAQ.

California property tax

Proposition 19 and the parent-child exclusion

For transfers on or after February 16, 2021, Proposition 19 replaced the broader parent-child and grandparent-grandchild exclusion. A family home may still avoid a full reassessment in some cases. In general, the home was the parent’s principal residence, the child makes it their principal residence, and the transfer fits a value limit the State Board of Equalization publishes and adjusts. The older exclusion for other real property was eliminated.

Transfers on or before February 15, 2021 followed the earlier rules. Filing timing, who counts as a child, and what happens above the value limit are on the Board’s chart. Do not plan a move-in, a rental, or a sibling buyout on a summary.

Straight answers

Questions heirs ask in the first month.

What should an heir do in the first 30 days after inheriting a house in California?

Start with papers and protection, not a sale. Locate the will, any trust, and the deed. Learn whether the home is headed for probate or trust administration. Secure the property and talk to the insurer, especially if it will sit vacant. Then name the real options — keep, rent, sell as-is, prepare and sell, or a sibling buyout — without choosing until a probate attorney and a CPA have looked at this house.

Does a will avoid probate in California?

A will says who should receive property. It does not, by itself, transfer title or skip court. In California, a home titled in the decedent’s name alone often still goes through probate. A probate attorney reads the will and the deed and tells you which process applies. This page is general education, not legal advice.

What is the difference between probate and trust administration?

Probate is a court process used to settle an estate and transfer assets that were in the decedent’s name. Trust administration is the successor trustee’s work when a home is held in a trust: notices, duties, and a transfer or sale without a full probate of that asset. They are different paths. Which one this house is on depends on title and the documents, not on a family’s preference.

What is a step-up in basis on an inherited home?

For federal income tax, the basis of property inherited from a decedent is generally its fair market value on the date of death, or the fair market value on the alternate valuation date if the estate qualifies and the personal representative elects it. People call that a step-up in basis. Exceptions exist, including for some property given to the decedent shortly before death. The IRS explains this in Publication 551 and its gifts-and-inheritances FAQ. A CPA applies it to a specific house.

How did Proposition 19 change the parent-child property tax exclusion?

For transfers on or after February 16, 2021, California replaced the broader parent-child exclusion with a narrower intergenerational exclusion. A family home may still avoid a full reassessment in some cases, generally when it was the parent’s principal residence, the child makes it their principal residence, and the transfer fits the value limit the State Board of Equalization publishes. The older exclusion for other real property was eliminated. Transfers on or before February 15, 2021 followed different rules. The Board of Equalization comparison chart is the official source. A tax professional applies it to this property.
