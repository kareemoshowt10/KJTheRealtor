About 25 minutes
01
Before you dial · five minutes

Get your numbers straight

Why this step exists: a retention representative decides how hard to work in about ninety seconds. Vague frustration gets a scripted apology. A tenure, a real number, and a competing quote get an offer. Everything below is built from the panel at the top of this page.
Why I start hereThis is the same thing I do before writing an offer on a house. Nobody negotiates well from partial information, and the other side can always tell which one you are within the first minute.
02
Call one · fifteen minutes

Go get a real number

You are shopping, not switching. Call a provider that actually serves your address and collect a complete offer. An advertised price is not an offer — the real number includes what happens after the promotion, plus equipment and installation.
Open with

“Hi — I'm a Spectrum customer paying about $___ a month for [speed] internet. I'm looking at my options. What can you offer at my address that matches or beats that?”

Then ask

“What does that price become after the promotional period ends, and how long is the promotional period?”

Then this

“Is there a contract or early termination fee? And is the router included, or is there a monthly equipment charge on top?”

Before you hang up

“Thank you — I'm comparing a couple of options this week. Can I have your name and a quote or reference number so I can come back to this?”

Why the post-promo price mattersHalf of these quotes are worse than what you already have once the promotion lapses. You are not collecting a plan to switch to. You are collecting a defensible number to say out loud on the next call.
03
Call two · the one that counts

Ask for retention, not support

Reaching the right department is most of the battle. Call 1-833-267-6094 or the number on your bill. When the menu asks why you're calling, say “cancel service” or “disconnect.” General support cannot authorize promotional pricing. Retention is measured on keeping you, so it can.
Your opener — the whole case in four sentences

“Hi — I've been a customer for [X] years and I genuinely like the service. My bill is now $___ a month for [speed]. I just got a quote from [competitor] at $___ with no contract. I'd rather not switch — but I can't justify the difference, so I need to know what you can do.”

They'll pull up the account and say some version of: “Let me see what I have available for you.” That's the sound of it working. Don't fill the silence.
Why warm beats angryThe representative did not raise your rate and has a limited set of offers to hand out. The pleasant caller with a real quote gets the better half of that set. I have never once seen hostility produce a bigger discount — in a phone queue or in an escrow.
When the first offer lands

“I appreciate you looking. I'm not in a rush — I'd like to find something that works. What's the best you can do on this account today?”

The first offer is rarely the last one. It's usually a small credit for six or twelve months.
Counter once — kindly, and only once

“That helps, thank you. Being straight with you: [competitor] is at $___. Can you get closer to that? Even ten dollars closer changes the math for me.”

If they say that's all they have

“I understand, and I don't want to make this difficult. Can you check whether there are any retention offers, loyalty credits, or promo codes sitting on my account? Sometimes there are things that don't come up automatically.”

The actual cancellation move — last, not first

“I understand your hands may be tied. I need to think about this seriously — can I put in a cancellation request today and take a few days to decide? I'd just like the option on file.”

What that last line really doesA pending disconnect frequently produces a callback from a save team with an offer nobody on the first call could reach. It's a request, not a resignation — you can withdraw it any time before the disconnect date. Say it calmly and mean it, or don't say it at all.
04
When they push back

Five answers, ready to read

You are not being difficult. You're a long-tenured customer asking for the rate a stranger gets today. Each of these is the reply to a thing they will actually say.
“That price is only for new customers.”
You say

“I understand — that's exactly why I'm calling. After [X] years I'd like the same consideration a new customer gets. Is there a loyalty rate on the account?”

“We can't match a competitor's pricing.”
You say

“I'm not asking you to match it exactly. I'm asking you to get me close enough that staying makes sense. What's the best you can do?”

“Your plan is already discounted.”
You say

“Can you tell me the standard rate, and the exact date my current discount expires? I'd like to know what I'm walking into.”

“You'd have to sign a contract.”
You say

“The competing offer has no contract. Is there a month-to-month version of this promotion?”

“I can lower it if you add TV or a mobile line.”
You say

“I don't want to add services. What's the lowest standalone internet rate you can put on this account?”

Nothing works and the rep won't move.
What to do

Thank them, hang up, and call back tomorrow. Different representative, different queue, different set of offers. This is the single most underused move on the page — and it costs nothing.

05
The line most people never touch

Kill the equipment fee

This is separate from the rate, and it is the easier win. A Wi-Fi equipment line runs roughly $14–18 a month for a router you can own outright for a one-time $80–150. Over three years that single line is often more than $500.
Say this while you still have them on the phone

“One more thing — I'm being charged $___ a month for Wi-Fi equipment. I'd like to return it and use my own compatible modem and router. Can you confirm that charge comes off once it's returned, and tell me where to return it?”

Why I push on this oneA rate discount expires. Owning your own equipment doesn't. It's the only part of this call whose savings compound every year without you having to call again.
06
Before you hang up

Get it in writing

A verbal rate that never reaches the bill is the most common way this falls apart. Ninety seconds here protects everything the previous twenty minutes earned.
The closing line

“Before we finish — can you email or text me confirmation with the new monthly total, the date it takes effect, and when the promotional rate ends? And may I have your name and a reference number for this call?”

OUTCOME A

They matched it

Accept, get the confirmation, save it somewhere you'll find it. Then set the reminder — this rate has an expiration date too.

OUTCOME B

Partial win

Take it if it saves $15 or more a month. Then call again in six months. Partial wins stack; most households run this twice before they land where they want.

OUTCOME C

They wouldn't move

You're holding a real quote already. Switch, or call back tomorrow for a different representative first. Either way you now know the actual price of staying.

Straight answers

The questions people actually text me after reading this.

Can you actually negotiate a Spectrum bill?

Often, yes. Retention representatives can apply promotional pricing and loyalty credits that general support has no authority to approve. What moves the conversation is a real competing quote for your address and a genuine willingness to schedule a disconnect. What doesn't move it is frustration alone — the person on the phone did not set your rate.

How much do people usually save?

A common outcome is $30 to $70 a month once expired promotional pricing is reinstated and the equipment rental is removed. It varies with your market, your tenure, your plan, and — more than anything else — whether a fiber competitor actually serves your address. Where there's no real competition, expect a smaller win.

Do I have to actually cancel?

No. Asking to place a cancellation request on file while you decide is usually what routes your account to a save team, and it can be withdrawn any time before the disconnect date. Don't make the threat if you aren't willing to let it sit there for a few days — the pause is the part that works.

I already bought my own router. Am I done?

Check the bill anyway. Equipment charges survive returns more often than they should. If the line is still there and you have the return receipt, call and ask for the credit retroactively, not just going forward.

When should I do this again?

Six months out, and again a month before the promotional rate expires. Promotional pricing lapses on a schedule; running this call twice a year is what keeps the bill from climbing back. There's a button above that will drop the reminder straight into your calendar.
